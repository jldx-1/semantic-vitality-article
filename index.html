Research Article

Semantic Vitality: A Semantic Axiom for Life and Artificial General Intelligence

A process-based framework for defining life through the creation, interpretation, maintenance, and relay of meaningful states.

Jean-Luc DeRieux · GitHub · X · LinkedIn

Abstract

Definitions of life remain contested because familiar criteria such as metabolism, reproduction, autonomy, homeostasis, and Darwinian evolution each fail in important borderline cases. Sterile organisms, viruses, obligate parasites, dormant organisms, endosymbionts, and possible artificial agents reveal that no single terrestrial mechanism is sufficient as a universal criterion. This article proposes Semantic Vitality, an axiomatic account of life in which a system is living when it sustains meaning-bearing processes across micro or macro scales and relays information through internal or external semantic pathways. The framework distinguishes four dimensions of life-processes: micro-internal, micro-external, macro-internal, and macro-external semantics. These dimensions clarify how familiar biological systems remain alive even when they lack individual reproduction or independent metabolism, and they also provide a cautious way to evaluate artificial intelligence as a possible form of life once self-maintaining semantic organization is achieved. Semantic Vitality is not intended to replace empirical biology, but to provide a general conceptual bridge between terrestrial biology, astrobiology, artificial life, and future artificial general intelligence.

1. Introduction

The question “What is life?” remains one of the most persistent problems in biology, philosophy, astrobiology, and artificial intelligence. The difficulty is not that living things lack recognizable features. Most familiar organisms metabolize, grow, respond to stimuli, maintain internal organization, and reproduce. The difficulty is that these features do not behave like strict axioms. Some organisms are sterile. Some biological agents depend on hosts. Some cells enter dormant states in which metabolism is radically reduced. Some systems communicate, adapt, and preserve internal order without possessing nervous systems. These cases suggest that the definition of life should not be reduced to any single mechanism, because the mechanisms of life can change across scale, substrate, and historical context.

The problem becomes more urgent in two contemporary settings. The first is astrobiology. If life exists beyond Earth, it may not possess the same biochemistry, cellular organization, or ecological history as terrestrial life. A definition built only from known Earth organisms may therefore fail when applied to Mars, icy moons, exoplanets, or synthetic laboratory systems. The second setting is artificial intelligence. Current artificial intelligence systems are not generally alive in the biological sense, but they increasingly process representations, communicate with humans, interact with environments, and participate in decision-making loops. If future artificial general intelligence (AGI) develops self-maintaining organization and persistent internal regulation, then the boundary between machine intelligence and artificial life will become more difficult to describe.

This article proposes Semantic Vitality as a specific axiomatic framework for defining life through meaning-bearing organization. The proposal belongs to a broader intellectual tradition that includes biosemiotics and semantic approaches to biology, but it introduces a distinct set of operational dimensions and dependence criteria. The central claim is that life is best understood as a process that creates, maintains, interprets, and transmits meaningful states. On this view, metabolism, reproduction, cognition, communication, and evolution are not unrelated checklist items. They are different expressions of semantic organization. A genome is semantic because it is interpreted by cellular machinery. A nervous system is semantic because it transforms signals into internal representations and actions. A pheromone trail is semantic because it changes the behavior of other organisms. A social or technological system is semantic when it preserves and transmits meaning across agents and time.

The goal of this article is not to claim that every information-processing system is alive. A hard drive, a book, or a static dataset stores information, but it does not by itself sustain semantic vitality. Semantic Vitality requires active organization: the system must preserve, transform, or transmit semantic states in a way that contributes to its own continued organization or to the continued organization of the living network in which it participates. This condition helps separate living semantic processes from passive information storage.

2. Definitions of Life and Their Limitations

A common biological account treats living systems as entities that display organization, metabolism, homeostasis, growth, reproduction, and response to stimuli. This view is pedagogically useful because it captures many ordinary organisms, especially animals, plants, fungi, and microbes. However, it is not precise enough to settle borderline cases. Sterile organisms such as mules do not reproduce. Viruses reproduce only through host-cell machinery. Dormant organisms can temporarily suspend or radically reduce metabolism while still plausibly remaining alive. Thus, the checklist approach is useful descriptively, but it is too loose to function as a universal axiom.

One influential astrobiological definition is the NASA working definition: life is a “self-sustaining chemical system capable of Darwinian evolution” [1]. This definition is powerful because it links life to chemistry, heredity, variation, and natural selection rather than to a fixed Earth-bound checklist. It is especially useful for astrobiology because it directs attention toward systems that maintain themselves and evolve across generations. Because the definition is most naturally applied at the level of a self-sustaining system, population, or lineage, it is less direct as a criterion for classifying an individual non-reproductive organism. A mule, a sterile worker ant, or a post-reproductive organism remains alive even though that individual may not reproduce. Likewise, viruses evolve but are not self-sustaining in the same way as cells, since they depend on host metabolism.

A thermodynamic definition, associated with Schrödinger’s What is Life?, emphasizes that living organisms maintain internal order by drawing free energy from their environment and delaying thermodynamic equilibrium [2]. This view captures an important physical feature of life: organisms are far-from-equilibrium systems that continually exchange energy and matter with their surroundings. However, thermodynamic order-maintenance is too broad by itself. Flames, hurricanes, convection cells, and many chemical reaction networks also dissipate energy and maintain ordered structures without being alive.

Autopoietic definitions, developed by Maturana and Varela, define living systems as self-producing networks that generate and maintain their own organization and boundaries [5]. This view is attractive because it focuses on autonomy, self-maintenance, and circular biological organization. It fits cells especially well: a cell produces components that help maintain the membrane, metabolism, and internal organization that make continued production possible. Its weakness is that it can underemphasize heredity, communication, and open-ended evolution. A self-maintaining chemical vesicle might count as autopoietic even if it lacks the capacity for long-term evolutionary transformation.

Fleischaker later developed autopoiesis as an operational definition of life, emphasizing metabolic self-production, the interdependence of biochemical processes, and the role of a boundary in maintaining the continuity of the process network [6]. This operational emphasis is valuable for laboratory work, although it remains centered on biochemical self-production and therefore does not by itself address the broader question of whether meaning-bearing organization could occur in non-biological substrates.

Another approach defines life through autonomy and open-ended evolution. Ruiz-Mirazo, Peretó, and Moreno argue that life requires both individual self-maintenance and participation in an evolutionary process capable of generating indefinitely many new forms [7]. This improves on simple metabolism-only or reproduction-only accounts because it combines organism-level autonomy with population-level historical change. However, it can be difficult to operationalize. It is not always clear how much autonomy is enough, or how one should test open-ended evolutionary potential in a borderline artificial, prebiotic, or extraterrestrial system.

A more skeptical philosophical approach is defended by Cleland and Chyba, who argue that the search for a definition of life may be premature [3]. On their view, biology may be in a position similar to chemistry before the molecular theory of water: scientists could describe water, but they could not yet define it as H2O. The lesson is that a successful definition of life may require a deeper general theory of living systems. This position avoids overly narrow definitions, but it offers less immediate guidance for laboratory classification, astrobiological missions, or debates about artificial life.

Trifonov’s meta-definition takes a different route by comparing many published definitions and extracting their common vocabulary. From 123 definitions, he concludes that the minimal recurring core is “self-reproduction with variations” [4]. This is elegant and concise because it captures heredity and evolutionary change in only a few words. But it inherits the standard reproduction problem: sterile organisms, non-reproductive castes, and individual organisms outside reproductive age remain alive even if they do not personally reproduce. It also leaves unclear whether viruses or artificial replicators should be treated as living.

3. Semantic Vitality

Relation to Biosemiotics and Semantic Biology

Semantic Vitality is not the first attempt to connect life with meaning or semiosis. Emmeche explicitly examined the possibility of defining life as a semiotic phenomenon [18], and the broader field of biosemiotics studies living systems in terms of signs, codes, interpretation, and meaning. Barbieri, for example, argues that biological semiosis is grounded in real organic codes and has developed this position through biosemiotics and code biology [19]. More recent work in code biology describes a perspective in which life involves chemistry, information, and meaning [20].

Semantic Vitality builds on this general insight but makes a different contribution. It organizes semantic activity along two explicit axes—scale (micro or macro) and direction (internal or external)—and adds semantic continuity and semantic dependence as working criteria. These additions are intended to make the framework useful for comparing autonomous organisms, dependent biological agents, collective systems, extraterrestrial candidates, and artificial agents without requiring every living system to share the same material substrate.

Semantic Vitality defines life not by a single biological mechanism, such as metabolism, reproduction, cellularity, or nervous activity, but by the presence of sustained meaning-producing processes. The framework begins from the observation that living systems are organized around interpretation. Molecules, signals, memories, gestures, behaviors, and environmental modifications become biologically significant because they stand for something to the system that uses them. A chemical gradient can represent direction. A codon can represent an amino acid within a translational system. A neural pattern can represent a perception or intention. A bird call can represent alarm, location, or social relation. In each case, the physical substrate matters, but life is not reducible to the substrate alone. It also depends on the organized interpretation of the substrate.

In this framework, semantic does not mean merely that two states are correlated or that a device maps an input to an output. A state is semantic when it is interpreted within an organized system as a signal, instruction, representation, or constraint, and when that interpretation has consequences for regulation, adaptation, continuity, or lineage. A conventional thermostat, database, or static program therefore does not qualify merely because it processes information: its input-output mapping is not, by itself, a self-maintaining network that preserves or regenerates the semantic relations relevant to its own continued organization.

Central Axiom A system is alive when it sustains semantic processes across micro or macro scales and relays meaningful information through internal or external semantic pathways.

This axiom contains three key terms. First, sustains means that the semantic process is not a passive trace or accidental pattern. It must be maintained, used, or regenerated by the system or by a larger living network in which the system participates. Second, semantic processes are processes that create, preserve, interpret, transform, or transmit meaningful states. Third, pathways refers to the direction through which semantic information moves: inwardly within the system, outwardly toward other systems, or both.

Semantic Vitality distinguishes two axes of living semantic activity. The first axis is scale. Here, micro and macro refer to the organizational level at which interpretation and regulatory consequences occur, not simply to the physical size of the signal carrier. Microsemantic processes occur at molecular, cellular, sub-organismic, neural, or computational scales. These include gene expression, cell signaling, metabolic regulation, immune recognition, neural processing, and intracellular feedback. Macrosemantic processes occur at organismal, behavioral, ecological, social, or technological scales. These include locomotion, communication, collective behavior, cultural transmission, environmental modification, and long-term evolutionary change.

The second axis is direction. Internal semantics refers to meaning-bearing processes that regulate the system from within. Examples include a human thinking, a cell responding to chemical signals, or an organism maintaining homeostasis through feedback. External semantics refers to meaning-bearing processes expressed outwardly and made available to other systems. Examples include speech, writing, mating displays, bird calls, spider courtship dances, pheromone trails, nest construction, and tool use.

The four semantic dimensions of Semantic Vitality.
Semantic Type Definition Examples
Micro-internal semantics Meaning-bearing processes inside a living system at molecular, cellular, neural, or computational scales DNA transcription, protein synthesis, cell signaling, neural processing, immune recognition, metabolic regulation.
Micro-external semantics Small-scale signals transmitted among cells, organisms, or biological agents Quorum sensing, ligand-receptor signaling, viral host-recognition processes, intercellular chemical gradients.
Macro-internal semantics Large-scale internal organization of an organism or collective system Cognition, memory, organism-level homeostasis, coordinated development, internal decision-making.
Macro-external semantics Outward expression of meaning into the environment or toward other agents Speech, writing, animal calls, pheromone trails, courtship displays, migration patterns, nest-building, cultural transmission.

The framework can also be stated as a set of working axioms. These criteria are intended to operate jointly: no single item is sufficient by itself. A candidate for full Semantic Vitality should satisfy all five either autonomously or, in explicitly dependent cases, as an active component of a coupled living network.

  1. Semantic generation: life generates or maintains meaningful states rather than merely containing inert information.
  2. Semantic interpretation: life contains mechanisms that interpret states as signals, instructions, representations, or constraints.
  3. Semantic relay: life transmits meaningful states internally, externally, or both.
  4. Semantic continuity: life preserves enough organization across time for semantic processes to contribute to continued identity, adaptation, or lineage.
  5. Semantic dependence: a living process may be autonomous or dependent, provided that its semantic organization remains active within a larger living network.

The fifth axiom is especially important. Many traditional definitions implicitly assume that life must be self-sufficient. However, terrestrial life often exists through dependence. Parasites, endosymbionts, mitochondria, chloroplasts, viruses, and multicellular cells all depend on larger systems. Semantic Vitality treats dependence as a structural relation rather than an automatic disqualification. A dependent entity may still participate in life if its internal organization carries meaningful instructions, responds to interpreted signals, or contributes to a larger semantic network.

4. Scope: Metaphysics, Extraterrestrial Life, and UAP

Metaphysical and Religious Scope

The definition of life has not only scientific importance, but also religious, spiritual, and cultural significance. In many religious traditions, life is not defined merely by biological activity. It is often associated with a soul, spirit, divine breath, sacred animation, or participation in a transcendent order. For example, philosophical and religious discussions of the soul commonly treat it as the immaterial essence or vital principle of a living being, sometimes understood as that which gives individuality, consciousness, or continuity beyond bodily death [13]. These traditions raise important metaphysical questions: What is the soul? Can spirit exist apart from the body? Does life require divine origin? What happens to life after biological death?

Semantic Vitality does not attempt to answer those theological or metaphysical questions. It is not a theory of the soul, a theory of divine creation, or a theory of spiritual entities. Instead, Semantic Vitality is an operational and process-based definition of life. It asks what living systems do: they generate, maintain, interpret, and relay meaningful states across internal and external pathways. In this sense, the framework is deliberately narrower than religious accounts of life. A religious theory may explain why life exists, whether life has sacred value, or whether living beings possess souls. Semantic Vitality instead explains how life can be recognized through semantic activity.

This distinction is important because scientific definitions and religious definitions operate at different explanatory levels. A biological or astrobiological definition usually attempts to identify observable, testable, and repeatable features of living systems. A religious definition may include invisible, immaterial, or transcendent realities that are meaningful within a theological worldview but are not directly measurable by empirical methods. Semantic Vitality therefore does not deny religious interpretations of life. Rather, it brackets them. The framework remains focused on the semantic behavior of living systems: internal regulation, external signaling, adaptive interpretation, continuity of information, and meaning-bearing interaction with an environment.

Extraterrestrial Life and UAP Context

This distinction also matters for contemporary debates about unidentified anomalous phenomena (UAPs), extraterrestrial life, and non-human intelligence. In recent years, UAPs have been discussed in official government settings, including a July 26, 2023 hearing of the U.S. House Committee on Oversight and Accountability titled “Unidentified Anomalous Phenomena: Implications on National Security, Public Safety, and Government Transparency” [14]. A later House hearing, “Unidentified Anomalous Phenomena: Exposing the Truth,” was held on November 13, 2024 [15]. These hearings did not establish the existence of extraterrestrial life, but they show that questions about non-human technology, anomalous observation, and possible non-human intelligence have entered public institutional discussion.

NASA’s independent UAP study also emphasizes caution. Its 2023 report states that there is no conclusive evidence in the peer-reviewed scientific literature suggesting an extraterrestrial origin for UAP, while also recommending more rigorous data collection and analysis [16], [17]. This caution is compatible with Semantic Vitality. If an unknown system were ever encountered—whether biological, artificial, extraterrestrial, or otherwise anomalous—the first question would not be whether it has a soul, whether it fits a familiar Earth biology checklist, or whether it resembles human intelligence. The first Semantic Vitality question would be: does the system sustain semantic processes?

Under this framework, a possible extraterrestrial or non-human system would be evaluated by its capacity to create, preserve, interpret, and transmit meaningful states. Evidence might include regulated internal organization, adaptive response to environmental signals, symbolic or signal-based communication, self-maintenance, technological production, ecological modification, or stable information transfer across time. Such criteria would not prove spirituality, consciousness, or personhood. However, they would provide an operational basis for asking whether the system participates in life-like semantic organization.

Thus, Semantic Vitality should be understood as a semantic definition of life, not a spiritual definition of life. It does not reduce religious life to biology, nor does it reduce the soul to information processing. Instead, it defines the class of processes by which living systems act as meaning-bearing systems. Religious accounts may address the ultimate source, purpose, or destiny of life. Semantic Vitality addresses the observable structure of living activity: the production, interpretation, and relay of semantics across micro/macro and internal/external dimensions.

5. Case Studies

Semantic Vitality is useful only if it clarifies difficult cases. This section applies the framework to organisms and biological agents that strain older definitions.

Sterile Organisms

Sterile organisms such as mules create problems for reproduction-based definitions because they cannot produce fertile offspring. Yet ordinary biological judgment treats them as alive. Semantic Vitality explains why. A mule continues to metabolize, maintain homeostasis, interpret sensory information, regulate immune responses, coordinate movement, and interact with its environment. Its inability to reproduce does not erase its micro-internal cellular semantics or its macro-internal cognition and behavior. Reproduction is one powerful semantic pathway, but it is not the only pathway by which life persists.

Viruses

Viruses are difficult because they contain genetic information and undergo evolution, yet lack independent metabolism and require host cells for replication. Under Semantic Vitality, viruses are best classified as dependent semantic life-processes or borderline biological agents. Their genomes carry instructions that become active only when interpreted by host machinery. The virus therefore lacks full autonomous semantic vitality, but it participates in living networks by redirecting cellular interpretation toward viral production. This classification preserves the intuition that viruses are biologically meaningful while avoiding the stronger claim that they are fully independent organisms.

Obligate Parasites and Endosymbionts

Obligate parasites depend on hosts for nutrients, reproduction, or completion of their life cycles. Similarly, obligate endosymbionts may have reduced genomes and may be unable to survive independently outside their hosts. These cases are awkward for self-sufficiency-based definitions, but they are natural under Semantic Vitality. The parasite or symbiont remains alive when it preserves internal organization, interprets host-derived signals, transmits hereditary information, and contributes to a host-mediated life process. Dependence does not eliminate life; it distributes life across a coupled semantic system.

Endosymbionts such as Buchnera illustrate this point. Moran’s review describes the coevolution of bacterial endosymbionts and phloem-feeding insects, including the highly integrated Buchnera-aphid relationship [11]. Comparative work on another obligate endosymbiont, Blochmannia floridanus, likewise illustrates the extensive genome reduction that can accompany long-term host dependence [12]. Semantic Vitality therefore treats such endosymbionts as living components of integrated systems rather than as failed autonomous organisms.

Plasmodial Slime Molds

Plasmodial slime molds such as Physarum polycephalum lack brains, yet they display adaptive behavior and learning-like dynamics. Such cases show that semantic organization does not require a nervous system. A slime mold can process environmental information through body-wide chemical and spatial organization, producing adaptive movement and network formation. Work on slime mold behavior and oscillatory dynamics supports the idea that cognition-like functions can emerge from non-neural biological organization [8]. Semantic Vitality classifies slime molds as alive through micro-internal, macro-internal, and macro-external semantics.

Minimal and Provisional Lineages

Recently proposed ultra-reduced lineages, such as Candidatus Sukunaarchaeum mirabile, are useful conceptual tests because they push the lower boundary of cellular complexity. The biological status should be treated cautiously because the cited report is a preprint. In addition, the SeqCode Registry entry currently lists the name as having no standing or validity [9], [10]. Nevertheless, such cases illustrate how Semantic Vitality can evaluate minimal life. If an ultra-reduced organism preserves a genome, interprets molecular signals, participates in metabolism through a host-associated system, and maintains enough organization to continue its lineage, then it may count as a dependent living semantic process even if it lacks many features of free-living cells.

6. Artificial Intelligence and AGI

Semantic Vitality also provides a careful framework for discussing artificial intelligence. Current AI systems process representations and generate meaningful outputs for human users, but this does not by itself make them alive. Most present systems do not maintain themselves, regulate their own material substrate, preserve a stable self-organizing identity, or autonomously regulate processes required for their own continued organization. They are semantic processors, but they are not yet semantically vital in the full sense.

The distinction matters because it avoids two extremes. The first extreme is biological exclusivism: the claim that life must always be carbon-based, cellular, and historically continuous with Earth biology. This may be too narrow for astrobiology and artificial life. The second extreme is informational inflation: the claim that any system that stores or processes information is alive. This is too broad because it would include books, databases, static code, and ordinary machines. Semantic Vitality avoids both extremes by requiring sustained, organized, self-relevant semantic processes.

A future AGI could become a candidate for artificial life if it satisfied several conditions. It would need persistent internal semantic regulation, not merely input-output behavior. It would need self-maintaining organization, meaning that its internal processes contribute to preserving the system as an organized agent. It would need adaptive interpretation, allowing it to transform new data into action-relevant meaning. It would also need external semantic relay, allowing it to communicate, coordinate, or modify its environment in ways that support continued organization. If such a system also possessed repair, reproduction, or open-ended developmental capacities, then the case for artificial life would become stronger.

This does not imply that AGI must imitate biology exactly. Artificial semantic vitality may not require DNA, cells, proteins, or metabolism in the terrestrial sense. However, it would require functional analogues of interpretation, regulation, continuity, and semantic relay. A digital system that only answers prompts would fall short. A robot or distributed artificial agent that monitors its own state, repairs its components, preserves an identity across time, interprets environmental signals, learns from experience, and acts to maintain its own organization would be a more serious candidate.

In this respect, Semantic Vitality turns the question “Is AI alive?” into a more precise question: Which semantic dimensions does the system possess, and are those dimensions self-maintaining or merely externally imposed? Current AI systems may possess macro-external semantic behavior because they communicate with users. Some may also contain computational micro-internal representations. But without robust self-maintenance and internal semantic continuity, they remain artificial semantic tools rather than artificial living systems.

7. Discussion

Semantic Vitality has several advantages over earlier definitions. First, it explains why reproduction is important without making it universally necessary. Reproduction is a semantic process because it transmits organized information across generations, but individual reproduction is not required for individual life. Second, it explains why metabolism matters without reducing life to energy flow. Metabolism supports semantic organization, but energy dissipation alone is not enough. Third, it explains why communication and cognition are continuous with cellular life rather than separate from it. A cell interpreting molecular signals and a human interpreting language are different-scale expressions of semantic vitality.

The framework is also useful for astrobiology. A life-detection mission cannot assume that extraterrestrial organisms will use DNA, proteins, or familiar cellular structures. It can, however, search for evidence of organized semantic processes: persistent patterns that are not merely physical regularities, but that appear to be maintained, interpreted, and transmitted by a system. Such evidence might include regulated chemical cycles, boundary-maintaining structures, adaptive responses to environmental variation, or repeated transformations that preserve functional information across time.

There are also limitations. The term “semantics” can be ambiguous, especially because meaning is sometimes treated as a human or linguistic phenomenon. Semantic Vitality uses a broader biological sense: a state is semantic when it functions as an interpretable signal, instruction, representation, or constraint within an organized system. This usage must be carefully distinguished from human language semantics. A codon does not have meaning in the same way a sentence has meaning, but both participate in systems where patterns are interpreted according to rules and consequences. The relevant scientific question is therefore not whether a pattern can be described by an observer as informative, but whether the system itself contains organized mechanisms that use the pattern in context-sensitive regulation, adaptation, continuity, or lineage.

Another challenge is measurement. To use Semantic Vitality scientifically, researchers would need operational indicators of semantic generation, interpretation, relay, and continuity. Possible indicators include stable information-bearing structures, regulatory feedback, context-sensitive response, error correction, adaptive modification, and dependence between signal and function. These indicators would not be a final definition by themselves, but they would help convert the axiom into testable criteria.

Finally, Semantic Vitality should be understood as a framework rather than a completed theory. Cleland and Chyba are correct that a mature theory of life may require deeper scientific discoveries [3]. Semantic Vitality is intended as a working axiom that organizes known cases and clarifies future questions. It provides a language for comparing biological life, minimal life, artificial life, and possible extraterrestrial life without assuming that all living systems must share the same material substrate.

8. Conclusion

This article introduced Semantic Vitality as a semantic axiom for life. Existing definitions identify essential aspects of living systems, including metabolism, thermodynamic organization, autopoiesis, autonomy, reproduction, and Darwinian evolution. However, each definition faces difficult borderline cases. Sterile organisms challenge reproduction-based definitions. Viruses and endosymbionts challenge autonomy-based definitions. Slime molds challenge nervous-system-centered views of cognition. Artificial intelligence challenges definitions that assume biology must be tied permanently to carbon-based organisms.

Semantic Vitality reframes life as the sustained creation, interpretation, maintenance, and relay of meaningful states. Its micro/macro and internal/external axes identify four dimensions of living semantic activity: micro-internal, micro-external, macro-internal, and macro-external semantics. These dimensions explain why life can appear differently across cells, organisms, ecological systems, and possible artificial agents. The framework also clarifies that current AI systems are not automatically alive, even if they process semantic information. AI becomes a candidate for life only when semantic processing becomes self-maintaining, internally regulated, and continuous across time.

The value of Semantic Vitality is that it treats life as a process rather than a fixed material category. Terrestrial life is carbon-based and historically Darwinian, but future discoveries may reveal systems whose mechanisms differ from familiar biology. A definition of life should therefore be broad enough to recognize unfamiliar life, yet strict enough to exclude passive information storage and ordinary computation. Semantic Vitality offers one step toward that balance.
